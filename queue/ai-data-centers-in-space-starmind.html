AI Data Centers in Space: The Plan for a Million Satellites

By Global Social Hub · December 3, 2026 · 5 min read
About 17,000 satellites circle Earth today. SpaceX now holds permission to add up to a million more — each one a small AI data center. (Image: Unsplash)

Key Facts

  • On November 25, 2026, the FCC authorized SpaceX to deploy up to 1,000,000 'Starmind' orbital compute satellites — the first authorization of its kind in US history. SpaceX had applied on January 30, 2026.
  • The satellites would orbit at 311 to 623 miles (500 to 1,002 km), draw constant solar power, and communicate by laser — with each other and with the Starlink network — flying in tight clusters so their chips can work together as one computer.
  • The license sets hard deadlines: a first satellite operating by November 2033, 100,000 satellites (10 percent) by November 2035, half the fleet by November 2038, and the full constellation by November 2040. Elon Musk has said a first demonstration could fly as early as late 2027.
  • For scale: only about 17,000 satellites are in orbit today, and the largest number of payloads ever carried by a single rocket is 143.
  • The approval is conditional. The FCC did not grant everything SpaceX requested — including a waiver of the usual end-of-life disposal rules for satellites in higher orbits.

One million. That is the number of satellites the US Federal Communications Commission has now authorized SpaceX to place in Earth orbit — not for internet service, but to work as flying data centers for artificial intelligence. The constellation is called Starmind, and the FCC's decision, announced on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, is the first authorization of its kind in American history.

Why move data centers off the planet?

AI's appetite for electricity is the driving force. On Earth, new data centers compete for grid connections, land and cooling water, and the FCC framed its approval as a response to 'rapidly growing demand' for AI computing power. Space offers a seductive alternative: above the atmosphere, solar panels drink in uninterrupted sunlight, and there is no neighborhood to object. SpaceX describes Starmind as 'capturing solar energy in space to power low-cost, high-performance AI compute for Earth.' The satellites would process data in orbit and beam the results down, linking to each other — and to the existing Starlink network — primarily by laser. Because a single satellite's computer is modest, SpaceX says the spacecraft will fly in tight formation clusters, pooling their chips into one coherent machine.

What the FCC actually approved

The license covers up to one million 'orbital compute modules' in shells at altitudes of 311 to 623 miles (500 to 1,002 km), in both 30-degree and sun-synchronous inclinations. It is not open-ended: SpaceX must have its first authorized satellite operating by November 25, 2033, reach 100,000 satellites by November 2035 and half a million by November 2038, and complete the fleet by November 2040 — or lose the unused authorization. SpaceX is manufacturing the Starmind spacecraft in partnership with Nvidia. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr called compute facilities 'essential to sustaining America's leadership in artificial intelligence.' The approval came with conditions, though: among other limits, the FCC declined SpaceX's request to waive the disposal rules that normally require dead satellites in higher orbits to be brought down to burn up, rather than parked in 'graveyard' orbits.

A million is not a typo

To grasp the scale, consider that humanity has placed only about 17,000 satellites in orbit in the entire space age — Starmind alone would multiply that roughly fifty-fold. The logistics are daunting: the most payloads ever carried by a single rocket is 143, so even at that record pace, a million satellites means thousands of launches. Critics raise the obvious questions — collision risk in crowded orbits, and a night sky streaked with hardware. As Reuters noted, the FCC largely considered those questions outside its remit. Elon Musk has suggested the first demonstration spacecraft could launch as early as the third quarter of 2027, with broader deployment from 2028 — years ahead of the license's own deadlines.

Permission is not deployment

A license is a starting gun, not a finish line. Whether SpaceX ever operates a million satellites is genuinely uncertain: the economics of orbital computing are unproven, the launch cadence required is unprecedented, and the deadlines stretch to 2040. What is certain is that the regulatory door is now open. A decade ago, a constellation of a few thousand satellites sounded like science fiction; today it is routine infrastructure. Starmind asks whether the same trick can be repeated two orders of magnitude larger — with the world's AI riding on the answer.
