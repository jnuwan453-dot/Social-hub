Turning Carbon Dioxide Into a Fuel Ingredient — Using Only Light

By Global Social Hub · December 1, 2026 · 4 min read
In a Rice University laboratory, visible light — not furnace heat — drove the reaction that split carbon dioxide into carbon monoxide. (Image: Unsplash)

Key Facts

  • Splitting carbon dioxide normally demands temperatures of 1,292 to 2,732 degrees Fahrenheit (700 to 1,500 °C) or intense electrical discharges — processes that can emit more carbon than they remove.
  • The Rice University system pairs light-harvesting copper nanoparticles with ruthenium reaction sites on cerium oxide, an 'oxygen sponge' that traps the freed oxygen so the reaction cannot run backwards.
  • Under blue light at a wavelength of 475 nanometers, it produced carbon monoxide at 15,000 micromoles per hour per gram of catalyst, and ran steadily for 238 minutes — nearly seven times longer than heat-driven controls, which degraded within 35 minutes.
  • The study was published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in November 2026, and the technology is licensed to Houston-based Syzygy Plasmonics to build scalable light-driven reactors.
  • Carbon monoxide is a key industrial feedstock for plastics and everyday chemicals — so waste CO2 could become a raw material worth paying for.

Carbon dioxide is the end of the line for carbon: a stable, satisfied molecule that would rather not react with anything. Breaking it apart has always meant brute force. Now a team at Rice University has shown how to do it with something far gentler — visible light — and in the process turned a waste gas into one of the chemical industry's most useful ingredients.

Why carbon dioxide is so hard to crack

Industry converts CO2 only at punishing cost. Conventional processes run at 1,292 to 2,732 degrees Fahrenheit (700 to 1,500 °C) or use intense electrical discharges — and because generating that heat often means burning fuel, the cure can emit as much carbon as the disease. Direct splitting has a second, sneakier problem: the moment a CO2 molecule is torn into carbon monoxide and oxygen, the freed oxygen tends to recombine with the product or attack the catalyst itself, shutting the reaction down. The prize is worth the fight, though. Carbon monoxide is a foundational feedstock for plastics and everyday industrial chemicals, so a clean way to make it from waste CO2 would let factories treat their emissions as inventory.

An antenna, a spark and an oxygen sponge

The Rice team's system, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, assigns each problem its own material. Copper nanoparticles act as microscopic antennas: when visible light strikes them, they harvest its energy and channel high-energy electrons into ruthenium reaction sites, which do the actual work of breaking CO2 apart. The third component is the clever one — cerium oxide, a material familiar from car catalytic converters, serves as an 'oxygen sponge.' It absorbs the freed oxygen into its own crystal structure, preventing recombination, and can later be regenerated with nitrogen gas under light, ready for the next cycle. The physics is extreme in miniature: the electrons driving the reaction are heated beyond 10,000 degrees Fahrenheit, while the catalyst as a whole reaches only a few hundred degrees. Cooling the reaction chamber to 50 degrees Fahrenheit (10 °C) actually improved production further, helping CO2 molecules stick to the catalyst surface.

What the numbers show

Driven by light rather than furnace heat, the system peaked under blue light at a wavelength of 475 nanometers, producing carbon monoxide at a rate of 15,000 micromoles per hour per gram of catalyst. Just as important is stamina: it held steady production for 238 minutes, nearly seven times longer than traditional heat-driven controls, which degraded within 35 minutes — and it yielded nearly three times more carbon monoxide than versions of the catalyst built without the cerium oxide sponge. 'What makes this advance so important is that it turns carbon dioxide straight into carbon monoxide, a major value-added product for industrial use,' said senior author Naomi Halas. 'That shift could make carbon capture genuinely profitable.'

From laser light to factory floors

The honest caveat is scale: today's results come from laser-powered laboratory reactors, not factory equipment. That translation is already assigned — the antenna-reactor technology has been licensed to Syzygy Plasmonics, a Houston clean-technology company co-founded by Rice researchers, which is engineering light-driven reactors designed to run on industrial lighting arrays. If the approach scales, the factories that today pay to emit carbon dioxide could one day sell it instead — split by light into the building blocks of the products they make.
