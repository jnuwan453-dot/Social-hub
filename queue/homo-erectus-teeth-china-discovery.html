The Teeth That Rewrote the Story of Homo Erectus

By Global Social Hub · December 2, 2026 · 4 min read
Homo erectus ranged across Africa and Asia for nearly two million years — far longer than modern humans have existed. Nine teeth from China just extended its story again. (Image: Unsplash)

Key Facts

  • Two studies published in Nature in November 2026 examined nine hominin teeth from the Baxian and Naxian caves in China's Guangxi region, about 9 miles (14 km) apart; eight of the teeth belong to Homo erectus.
  • The teeth date to between 324,000 and 184,000 years ago — meaning Homo erectus persisted on mainland Asia up to 216,000 years longer than the previously accepted timeline, which had the species disappearing there around 400,000 years ago.
  • The caves stood in tropical rainforest — the first evidence that Homo erectus could live in that demanding environment, overturning a common theory about why the species died out.
  • Researchers recovered the ancient protein ameloblastin from five of the teeth. A ninth, mystery tooth matches no known hominin — and Denisovans are the leading candidate for its owner.
  • Homo erectus arose in Africa about 1.89 million years ago and became the first human ancestor to spread widely across the Old World.

Teeth are the time capsules of human evolution: harder than bone, they survive when everything else crumbles. Now nine of them, pulled from two limestone caves in southern China, have forced scientists to rewrite the final chapter of Homo erectus — one of our closest extinct relatives — extending its story by more than 200,000 years and moving it into an environment nobody thought it could handle.

Nine teeth in two caves

The teeth come from the Baxian and Naxian caves in China's Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, about 9 miles (14 km) apart. Using CT scanning and close physical examination, the teams behind two papers in Nature determined that eight of the nine teeth belonged to Homo erectus — the upright, large-brained species that arose in Africa about 1.89 million years ago and was first recognized from fossils found by Dutch anatomist Eugène Dubois in the early 1890s. The researchers went further than shape: from five teeth they recovered traces of ameloblastin, an ancient protein, and in the four confirmed Homo erectus teeth it matched a version previously seen only in older Asian fossils of the species.

A much later farewell

The accepted story had Homo erectus vanishing from mainland Asia roughly 400,000 years ago. Multiple dating methods applied to the cave teeth say otherwise: they are between 324,000 and 184,000 years old. In other words, Homo erectus may have persisted in southern China as recently as 184,000 years ago — surviving up to 216,000 years longer than textbooks claimed. That is not a rounding error; it is a second lifetime for the species, unfolding in Asia while, elsewhere, the ancestors of modern humans were still evolving.

Rainforest pioneers

The setting makes the finding stranger. Around the caves, the ancient environment was tropical rainforest — dense, wet and short on the open-country game that early humans were thought to depend on. These are the first fossils showing Homo erectus living in rainforest at all. That turns a common extinction theory on its head: rainforest had been considered a frontier that only much later, more adaptable humans could cross. Instead, it seems Homo erectus was considerably more flexible — ecologically and probably behaviorally — than its reputation as a rigid, primitive ancestor suggests.

The tooth that doesn't fit

Then there is the ninth tooth. It carried the same ancient protein as the others, but its ameloblastin matched no known hominin — not Homo erectus, not us. Co-author Frido Welker, a molecular anthropologist at the University of Copenhagen, says the Denisovans — the mysterious lineage known mostly from a Siberian cave and a Tibetan jawbone, which ranged across Asia roughly 400,000 to 30,000 years ago — 'would be the first option to consider.' For now the tooth keeps its secret. But that is the pattern of this discovery: the more precisely scientists can read these small, hard fragments, the more the human family tree turns out to have extra branches — and longer ones.
