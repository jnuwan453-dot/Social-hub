The Year Clean Energy Passed Coal

By Global Social Hub · December 4, 2026 · 4 min read
The world added a record 692 gigawatts of renewable capacity in 2025 — and, for the first time, clean sources generated more electricity than coal. (Image: Unsplash)

Key Facts

  • Ember's Global Electricity Review found that renewables produced 33.8 percent of the world's electricity in 2025, ahead of coal at 33.0 percent — the first time in history that clean sources generated more power than coal.
  • According to IRENA, total global renewable capacity reached 5,149 gigawatts by the end of 2025, after a record 692 gigawatts was added in a single year — growth of 15.5 percent, the fastest ever recorded.
  • Solar power is the giant: 2,392 gigawatts installed worldwide (47 percent of all renewables), ahead of hydropower at 1,296 gigawatts and wind at 1,291 gigawatts. Solar and wind together made up 96.8 percent of all new renewable capacity added in 2025.
  • Solar panel prices have fallen by more than 90 percent since 2010, and the International Energy Agency expects solar alone to supply about 20 percent of world electricity by 2030, up from roughly 8 percent in 2025.
  • The hard part is now the grid: battery storage stood at only about 100 gigawatts at the end of 2025 and must grow six to ten times, alongside roughly $600 billion a year of investment in electricity grids.

For more than a century, coal was the backbone of the world's electricity — the fuel of industrial revolutions on three continents. In 2025, according to Ember's Global Electricity Review, it was quietly overtaken: renewable sources generated 33.8 percent of global electricity, against coal's 33.0 percent. The margin is slim. The symbolism is enormous. For the first time since the dawn of the electrical age, humanity got more of its power from sun, wind, water and other clean sources than from burning coal.

Solar did the heavy lifting

The crossover was built panel by panel. Data from the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) show that global renewable capacity reached 5,149 gigawatts by the end of 2025, after a record 692 gigawatts was added in a single year — growth of 15.5 percent, the fastest ever recorded, with renewables accounting for 85.6 percent of all new power capacity of any kind. Solar photovoltaic power is the story within the story: 2,392 gigawatts installed (47 percent of all renewables), with a record 511 gigawatts added in 2025 alone. Wind added its own record 159 gigawatts, reaching 1,291 gigawatts, just behind hydropower's 1,296. Together, solar and wind supplied 96.8 percent of all new renewable capacity. The economics explain the stampede: solar panel prices have fallen by more than 90 percent since 2010, making sunlight the cheapest source of new electricity in most of the world, and the IEA projects solar will supply about 20 percent of global electricity by 2030.

China's outsized role

No account of the milestone is honest without China, which has installed roughly 1,400 gigawatts of renewable capacity — more than the next five countries combined — and manufactures over 80 percent of the world's solar panels. That concentration drove global costs down, even as it raised supply-chain anxieties in Western capitals. It also introduces the plot twist in the IEA's Renewables 2026 outlook: after 21 consecutive years of records, global renewable additions are forecast to dip about 11 percent in 2026, mainly because China's solar build-out is slowing as support shifts from fixed payments to competitive auctions. Even so, 2026 would remain the second-biggest year in history, with growth in India, Europe, the United States and emerging markets cushioning the slowdown — and the IEA still expects some 4,600 gigawatts of new capacity between 2025 and 2030.

Winning generation, wrestling the grid

Passing coal in generation does not finish the job; it relocates it. Sunshine and wind arrive on their own schedules, while demand peaks in the evening. Battery storage — the technology meant to bridge that gap — stood at only about 100 gigawatts worldwide at the end of 2025 and, by industry estimates, must scale six to ten times to support grids where renewables supply half or more of all power. The wires themselves are the other bottleneck: the IEA estimates grids need around $600 billion in investment every year through 2030, and in major markets including the United States, the United Kingdom and Brazil, new projects already wait two to five years just to connect. The age of arguing about whether clean energy can out-generate coal is over. The argument now — over storage, transmission and time — is about how fast the rest of the system can catch up.
