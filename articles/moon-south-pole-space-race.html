The New Space Race: Why Everyone Wants the Moon's South Pole

By Global Social Hub · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
The lunar south pole: shadowed craters there may hold water ice, while nearby ridges enjoy near-constant sunlight. (Image: Unsplash)

Key Facts

  • Craters at the lunar south pole have floors in permanent shadow, where temperatures plunge below -300°F (-184°C) and water ice can survive for billions of years.
  • As of late October 2026, 78 countries had signed the Artemis Accords, the U.S.-led framework for lunar exploration (NASA).
  • China's Chang'e-7 mission — an orbiter, lander, rover and a hopping probe — will hunt for ice near Shackleton Crater; its planned 2026 launch was postponed to 2027.
  • India landed Chandrayaan-3 near the south pole in August 2023, and its Chandrayaan-5 (LUPEX) rover mission with Japan was approved in March 2025 for launch no earlier than 2028.
  • Water ice is valuable because it can be split into oxygen for breathing and hydrogen and oxygen for rocket fuel.

Half a century after Apollo, the world's space powers are heading back to the Moon — and remarkably, they are all heading for the same small patch of it. The lunar south pole, a rugged landscape of craters and ridges at the bottom of the Moon, has become the most sought-after destination in space. The reason is not scenery. It is ice.

Water where the sun never shines

The Moon's axis is barely tilted, so at the south pole the sun never climbs high in the sky — it skims the horizon. Deep crater floors there have not seen direct sunlight for billions of years, making them some of the coldest places in the solar system. Spacecraft instruments — including India's Chandrayaan-1 orbiter in 2008, and a NASA experiment in 2009 that deliberately crashed a stage into the crater Cabeus and found water in the spray — have confirmed that these cold traps hold water ice, delivered over the ages by comets and asteroids.

Ice changes everything about living on the Moon. Melted, it is drinking water. Split apart with electricity, it becomes oxygen to breathe — plus hydrogen and oxygen, the most powerful rocket fuel combination there is. A base that makes fuel at the south pole could one day supply missions heading to Mars without hauling every drop up from Earth. And just a few miles from the frozen craters, high ridges stand in sunlight almost all year round — ideal for solar power. Ice in the dark, energy in the light, side by side: no other spot on the Moon offers both.

Two alliances, one destination

The race has organized itself into two loose camps. The United States leads through the Artemis program and the Artemis Accords, a set of principles for peaceful, transparent exploration that, according to NASA, 78 countries had signed as of late October 2026. NASA's Artemis III mission aims to land astronauts near the south pole, while American robotic landers scout the region in advance.

China, working with Russia on a planned International Lunar Research Station, is advancing methodically through its Chang'e program. Its next step, Chang'e-7, is a four-part mission — orbiter, lander, rover and a small hopping probe designed to leap into shadowed craters and analyze the ice directly — targeting the rim of Shackleton Crater, right at the pole. The mission was being prepared for launch in 2026, but China postponed the flight to 2027. A follow-up, Chang'e-8, is planned later this decade to test turning lunar soil and ice into useful resources, ahead of a Chinese crewed landing targeted before 2030.

India's southern strategy

India earned its place in this race in August 2023, when Chandrayaan-3 touched down closer to the south pole than any spacecraft before it. New Delhi is pressing on: Chandrayaan-4 is being built to collect samples near the pole and fly them back to Earth. And in March 2025 India formally approved Chandrayaan-5 — also called LUPEX — a joint mission with Japan. Under the implementing arrangement signed in August 2025, Japan will supply the rocket and a 770-pound (350 kg) rover designed to drill as deep as 5 feet (1.5 m) in search of ice, while India provides the lander. Launch is planned no earlier than 2028.

Why it matters now

Unlike Apollo, this race is not about flags. Whoever first learns to find, extract and use polar ice gains the fuel depot, the power supply and the practical experience every deeper mission will need. The rules are still being written — the Artemis Accords on one side, China's open invitation to join its station on the other — and the mining technology is still unproven. But the destination is no longer in doubt. The south pole of the Moon is where the next era of exploration will be won or lost.
