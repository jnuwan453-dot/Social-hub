How GPS Works: The Satellites That Guide Your Day

By Global Social Hub · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
A navigation fix starts far above the clouds: GPS satellites circle the planet twice a day, broadcasting time signals to billions of receivers. (Image: Unsplash)

Key Facts

  • GPS is operated by the U.S. Space Force. The constellation is built around a baseline of 24 satellites, and 32 were listed as operational in 2026 (U.S. Coast Guard Navigation Center).
  • The satellites orbit about 12,550 miles (20,200 km) above Earth in six orbital planes, circling the planet roughly twice a day.
  • Each satellite carries atomic clocks and broadcasts its position and the exact time; your receiver works out where you are by timing how long the signals take to arrive.
  • A GPS-enabled smartphone is typically accurate to within about 16 feet (4.9 m) under open sky (GPS.gov).
  • All ten GPS III satellites had been launched by April 2026; they offer roughly three times better accuracy and eight times better anti-jamming than the previous generation.

The blue dot on your phone feels like magic, but it is really an exercise in very precise timekeeping. The Global Positioning System — GPS — is a fleet of satellites, run by the U.S. Space Force and free for anyone on Earth to use, that does one simple thing relentlessly: it broadcasts what time it is. Your phone turns those time signals into a position accurate to a few steps. Here is how.

Position from pure timing

Each GPS satellite circles Earth at about 12,550 miles (20,200 km) altitude, completing an orbit roughly every 12 hours, and carries atomic clocks of extraordinary precision. It continually transmits a signal stamped with the exact time it was sent and the satellite's own position. Your receiver picks up that signal and notes the arrival time. Because radio waves travel at the speed of light — about 186,000 miles (300,000 km) per second — even a tiny delay reveals distance: a signal that left 0.07 seconds ago came from roughly 12,550 miles away.

Knowing your distance from one satellite puts you somewhere on a giant sphere. A second satellite narrows it to a circle; a third, to two points, one of which is usually absurd (deep space or inside the Earth). This is trilateration — triangulation's quieter cousin — and it is why GPS receivers listen to as many satellites as they can see.

Why your phone needs a fourth satellite

There is a catch. Satellite clocks are atomic; your phone's clock is not, and at light speed a clock error of one millionth of a second becomes a position error of about 1,000 feet (300 m). The fix is elegant: a signal from a fourth satellite gives the receiver enough information to solve for its own clock error as well as its latitude, longitude and altitude. Every GPS fix is really four answers at once — three for place, one for time.

GPS even has to outsmart Einstein. Because the satellites move fast and sit in weaker gravity than we do, their clocks gain about 38 millionths of a second per day relative to clocks on the ground — an effect predicted by relativity. Left uncorrected, it would throw positions off by roughly 6 miles (10 km) a day. Engineers build the correction in, making GPS an everyday, pocket-sized proof that relativity is real.

The constellation today: GPS III

The United States commits to keeping at least 24 GPS satellites operational, and in practice flies more: the U.S. Coast Guard Navigation Center listed 32 operational satellites in 2026, spread across six orbital planes so that at least four are visible from almost anywhere, at any time. The fleet is a mix of generations, topped by GPS III, built by Lockheed Martin. The tenth and final GPS III satellite launched in April 2026. These newest spacecraft broadcast a modern civil signal called L1C — designed to work smoothly with Europe's Galileo and other systems — and deliver roughly three times the accuracy and eight times the resistance to jamming of the satellites they replace. A follow-on generation, GPS IIIF, is in production.

A utility the world runs on

GPS began as a military system — the first satellite launched in 1978, and the constellation was declared fully operational in 1995 — but its timing signals now quietly synchronize power grids, cell networks, stock exchanges and farm equipment, quite apart from guiding aircraft, ships and lost drivers. All of it rests on a simple, beautiful idea: if you know exactly what time it is in orbit, you know exactly where you are on Earth.
