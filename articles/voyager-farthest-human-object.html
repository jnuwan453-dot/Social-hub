Voyager 1: The Farthest Human-Made Object

By The Civic Brief · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
Jupiter, photographed by NASA's Cassini spacecraft. Voyager 1 flew past Jupiter in 1979 on its way out of the solar system. (Image: NASA)

Key Facts

  • Voyager 1 launched on September 5, 1977 — and is still operating today.
  • It is more than 16 billion miles (25.9 billion km) from Earth, farther than any human-made object.
  • On November 18, 2026, it became the first spacecraft one full light-day from Earth — its radio signal now takes a full 24 hours to reach us, one way.
  • In 2012 it became the first spacecraft to enter interstellar space.
  • It carries the Golden Record — sounds and images of Earth chosen to represent humanity.

Somewhere beyond the planets, in the cold dark between the stars, a machine the size of a small car is still doing its job. Voyager 1 was built with 1970s technology — its computer has a tiny fraction of the power of a modern phone — yet after nearly five decades it remains in contact with Earth from a distance so large it is hard to picture: more than 16 billion miles (25.9 billion km).

A grand tour of the giants

Voyager 1 and its twin, Voyager 2, were launched in 1977 to take advantage of a rare alignment of the outer planets that happens only once every 176 years. Voyager 1 flew past Jupiter in 1979, returning the first detailed images of the giant planet and its volcanic moon Io, then swung by Saturn in 1980 for a close look at its rings and the hazy moon Titan.

That Saturn encounter bent Voyager 1's path upward, out of the plane of the planets, on a one-way journey out of the solar system. Its cameras were eventually turned off to save power — but not before one last, famous photograph.

The Pale Blue Dot

On February 14, 1990, from a distance of about 3.7 billion miles (6 billion km), Voyager 1 looked back and photographed Earth: a single pale blue pixel suspended in a band of scattered light. Astronomer Carl Sagan, who had requested the image, wrote that everyone who ever lived spent their lives "on a mote of dust suspended in a sunbeam." It remains one of the most powerful photographs ever taken.

Into interstellar space

In August 2012, Voyager 1 crossed the heliopause — the boundary where the Sun's wind gives way to the space between the stars — becoming the first human-made object in interstellar space. Voyager 2 followed in 2018. Both spacecraft are now measuring particles, magnetic fields and cosmic rays in a region no probe had ever sampled directly.

Distance changes everything about running the mission. Commands from NASA's Deep Space Network take a full 24 hours to arrive, travelling at light speed, and the reply takes just as long. The signal that reaches Earth is unimaginably faint — far weaker than the power of a watch battery — yet engineers still decode it, most recently working around a serious computer fault in 2023–2024 that briefly turned the spacecraft's transmissions into gibberish before a clever fix restored its science data.

November 2026: one light-day from home

On November 18, 2026, Voyager 1 passed a milestone no spacecraft had ever reached: one light-day from Earth. At 16.1 billion miles (25.9 billion km) away, even a signal moving at the speed of light needs an entire day to make the trip — and another day for any reply to come back. NASA also reported in November 2026 that the spacecraft had recently been sending only a basic carrier signal while engineers investigated a technical problem — a sobering reminder of how fragile our link to this 49-year-old messenger has become, and how remarkable it is that it answers at all.

Running on empty, gracefully

Voyager 1's nuclear power source loses about 4 watts every year. To stretch the mission, NASA has gradually switched off instruments and heaters, keeping only the essentials. Engineers believe a few instruments may keep working into the early 2030s. When the power finally runs out, Voyager will fall silent — but it will not stop.

Carrying the Golden Record, with greetings in 55 languages, music from Bach to Chuck Berry, and images of life on Earth, Voyager 1 will drift among the stars for billions of years — long outliving the planet that built it. It is, quite literally, humanity's message in a bottle, thrown into the cosmic ocean.
