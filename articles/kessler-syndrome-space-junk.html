Space Is Full: The Kessler Syndrome Explained

By Global Social Hub · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
Low Earth orbit is now home to thousands of satellites — and millions of pieces of debris. (Image: Unsplash)

Key Facts

  • About 40,000 objects are tracked in Earth orbit — and ESA estimates the true total larger than 4 inches (10 cm) is over 50,000 (ESA Space Environment Report, 2025).
  • An estimated 1.2 million objects larger than 0.4 inches (1 cm) — each capable of destroying a satellite — are too small to track (ESA, 2025).
  • Debris in low Earth orbit travels at up to 17,500 mph (28,000 km/h) — about ten times faster than a bullet.
  • In 2009, a working Iridium satellite collided with a dead Russian satellite, creating more than 2,000 new tracked fragments.

Look up on a clear night and space seems empty and calm. In reality, the region a few hundred miles above your head is the busiest — and messiest — highway humanity has ever built. Dead satellites, spent rocket stages and fragments from past collisions are all circling the planet, and every one of them is moving fast enough to punch through solid metal.

A prediction from 1978

The problem has a name: the Kessler Syndrome. In 1978, NASA scientist Donald J. Kessler warned that as the number of objects in orbit grows, collisions become more likely — and each collision creates thousands of new fragments, which go on to cause still more collisions. Left unchecked, the process becomes a chain reaction that could make some orbits too dangerous to use for generations.

We have already seen the first links of that chain. In 2009, the active Iridium 33 communications satellite smashed into Cosmos 2251, a retired Russian satellite, at a closing speed of roughly 26,000 mph (42,000 km/h). Both spacecraft were destroyed instantly, scattering more than 2,000 trackable fragments — every one of them now a hazard to other satellites. China's 2007 anti-satellite test and Russia's 2021 test added thousands more.

Why small pieces are a big threat

At orbital speeds, size is misleading. A fragment just 0.4 inches (1 cm) across carries the energy of a hand grenade. A fleck of paint once chipped a window on the Space Shuttle. The International Space Station has performed dozens of avoidance maneuvers since 1999, shifting its orbit to dodge tracked debris, and crews have occasionally sheltered in their return capsules when a fragment passed uncomfortably close.

Ground radar and telescopes can track objects down to about 4 inches (10 cm) in low orbit, which is why the catalog of about 40,000 tracked objects matters. The far greater danger is the estimated 1.2 million pieces larger than 0.4 inches (1 cm) that nobody can track — satellites simply have to be built tough and hope to be lucky.

Fighting back

The good news is that the world has started to take the problem seriously. The U.S. Federal Communications Commission now requires most new satellites in low orbit to be removed within five years of finishing their mission, down from the old 25-year guideline. Operators increasingly design satellites to burn up completely on re-entry, and large constellations maneuver automatically to avoid collisions.

The next step is active cleanup. The European Space Agency's ClearSpace-1 mission aims to capture a piece of rocket debris and drag it down to burn up in the atmosphere later this decade — the first attempt to remove junk that is already up there. Companies and agencies are also testing nets, harpoons, lasers and robotic arms for the same job.

Why it matters to you

Weather forecasts, GPS navigation, banking networks, farming and emergency services all depend on satellites in the very orbits most at risk. Protecting those orbits is not just an engineering problem — it is about keeping the invisible infrastructure of modern life working. The Kessler Syndrome is not inevitable, but avoiding it means treating orbit the way we learned, too late, to treat the oceans: as a shared space that nobody can afford to fill with junk.
