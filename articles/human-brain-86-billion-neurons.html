The Human Brain: 86 Billion Neurons at Work

By The Civic Brief · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
A model of the human brain. The real thing packs about 86 billion neurons into roughly 3 pounds (1.4 kg) of tissue. (Image: Unsplash)

Key Facts

  • The adult human brain contains about 86 billion neurons and roughly 85 billion other cells — counted directly for the first time in 2009 (Azevedo et al., Journal of Comparative Neurology).
  • About 69 billion of those neurons sit in the cerebellum; the cerebral cortex holds about 16 billion.
  • The brain weighs about 3 pounds (1.4 kg) but uses roughly 20% of the body's resting energy.
  • In May 2025, the FDA cleared the first blood test to help diagnose Alzheimer's disease — Fujirebio's Lumipulse plasma test.
  • In its validation study of 499 patients, 91.7% of positive and 97.3% of negative results agreed with PET scans or spinal-fluid tests (FDA).

Right now, as you read this sentence, an electrical storm is moving through your head. About 86 billion neurons are firing, passing signals across trillions of connections, weighing options, forming memories and quietly running your body in the background — all on roughly the electrical power of a dim light bulb. The human brain is the most complex object science has ever studied, and some of its most basic numbers were only pinned down this century.

Counting the uncountable

For decades, textbooks repeated that the brain contains 100 billion neurons and ten times as many support cells, called glia. The numbers sounded authoritative — but nobody had actually counted. In 2009, a team led by Suzana Herculano-Houzel in Brazil did, using a method nicknamed the "brain soup" technique: brain tissue is dissolved into a uniform liquid of cell nuclei, and samples of the soup are counted under a microscope. The result, published by Frederico Azevedo and colleagues, was 86.1 billion neurons — plus about 84.6 billion non-neuronal cells, a ratio of roughly one to one, not ten to one. The count also held a surprise: the cerebellum, the compact structure at the back of the brain, holds about 69 billion neurons, while the celebrated cerebral cortex — the seat of language and reasoning — holds about 16 billion.

A small organ with a huge fuel bill

The brain weighs only about 3 pounds (1.4 kg), roughly 2% of body weight, yet it consumes about 20% of the body's energy at rest — around 20 watts, burned mostly on signaling. Its fastest neurons relay impulses at up to 268 mph (432 km/h), and its neurons connect through an estimated 100 trillion synapses, adjustable junctions that strengthen or weaken with experience. That adjustability is learning itself: every skill you practice is, physically, a rewiring of this network.

Reading the brain in a drop of blood

When the network breaks down, the consequences are devastating. Alzheimer's disease slowly destroys memory and thinking, and nearly 7 million Americans are living with it, according to the FDA. Confirming the disease used to require an expensive PET brain scan or a spinal tap to sample cerebrospinal fluid. On May 16, 2025, the FDA cleared the first blood test to help make that diagnosis: Fujirebio's Lumipulse G pTau217/ß-Amyloid 1-42 Plasma Ratio. It measures two proteins in blood whose ratio tracks the amyloid plaques that are a hallmark of Alzheimer's. In the 499-patient study behind the clearance, 91.7% of positive results and 97.3% of negative results matched PET scans or spinal-fluid tests. The FDA is careful about limits — the test is for adults aged 55 and older who already show symptoms, and it aids diagnosis rather than replacing clinical judgment. Even so, a simple blood draw promises earlier, cheaper and far more accessible answers.

Why the numbers matter

Knowing the brain contains 86 billion neurons is more than trivia. Cell counts anchor computer models of the brain, guide comparisons with other species, and help researchers understand what diseases actually destroy. And as blood tests begin to reveal the brain's hidden chemistry years before symptoms peak, the organ that science once could not even count is slowly becoming one it can measure.
