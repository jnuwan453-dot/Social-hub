Robots Report for Work: The Machines Joining the Workforce

By The Civic Brief · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
Robots have worked in factories for decades. AI is now giving them eyes, judgment — and new workplaces. (Image: Unsplash)

Key Facts

  • More than 4.28 million industrial robots were operating in factories worldwide by the end of 2023 (IFR).
  • Over 540,000 new industrial robots were installed in 2023 — the third year in a row above half a million.
  • Amazon employs more than 750,000 robots across its operations, working alongside about 1.5 million people.
  • Humanoid robots such as Digit and Figure 02 are now being tested in real warehouses and car factories.

The robot workforce is not coming — it is already here, and it recently passed a remarkable milestone. According to the International Federation of Robotics (IFR), more than 4.28 million industrial robots were at work in factories around the world by the end of 2023, with over 540,000 new machines installed in that year alone. What is changing now is where robots work, what they look like, and how much they can figure out for themselves.

From cages to collaboration

Traditional factory robots were powerful, fast and blind — kept behind fences because they would repeat the same motion whether or not a person stood in the way. The new generation is different. Sensors, cameras and AI software let modern robots see their surroundings, adjust their grip, and work safely beside people. "Cobots" — collaborative robots — now assemble electronics, pack boxes and inspect products shoulder-to-shoulder with human colleagues.

Nowhere is the shift clearer than in logistics. Amazon, the world's most automated retailer, runs more than 750,000 robots across its fulfillment centers: mobile drive units that glide under shelves and bring them to workers, robotic arms that sort packages, and systems that pack orders. The company says the machines take on the most repetitive, physically punishing tasks — while it has also created entirely new job categories, from robot technicians to fleet monitors, for the people who keep them running.

Enter the humanoids

The newest recruits look like us. Humanoid robots — built to use the stairs, doors, tools and shelves designed for people — have begun real-world trials. Agility Robotics' Digit, a two-legged machine that carries boxes, has been tested in Amazon warehouses and works commercially at a GXO logistics site. Figure AI's humanoid robots have been trialled on BMW's production line in South Carolina, and Tesla is developing its Optimus robot for factory work.

The logic is simple: the world is built for human bodies, so a machine shaped like one can slot into existing workplaces without rebuilding them. The challenge is equally simple — walking, balancing and handling unpredictable objects are things toddlers master and robots still find genuinely hard.

What it means for human jobs

Every wave of automation has raised the same fear, and the honest answer from the data is "both." Robots do replace specific tasks, especially repetitive and dangerous ones. But historically they have also created work that did not exist before — somebody designs, builds, programs, supervises and repairs 4 million machines. The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs research finds employers expect automation to change the mix of jobs far more than the total: roles shrink in routine manual work and grow in technology, care, and skilled trades.

The practical lesson of the robot era so far is that machines take tasks, not whole professions — and the workers who thrive are those whose jobs shift toward judgment, creativity and human contact, the things robots remain worst at. The robots have reported for work. The task for the rest of us is deciding what work is worth keeping human.
