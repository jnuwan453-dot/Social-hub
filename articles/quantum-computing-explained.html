Quantum Computing Explained: Beyond Bits

By Global Social Hub · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
An IBM Quantum System One. Inside machines like this, qubits are kept colder than deep space to protect their fragile quantum states. (Image: Wikimedia Commons)

Key Facts

  • A qubit can hold a blend of 0 and 1 at once (superposition), and qubits can be linked by entanglement — the two effects quantum computers exploit.
  • Google's Willow chip (December 2024) packs 105 qubits and demonstrated 'below threshold' error correction — errors fell as more qubits were added (Nature).
  • Willow ran a benchmark in under 5 minutes that Google estimates would take a leading supercomputer 10 septillion years.
  • Microsoft's Majorana 1 (February 2025) carries 8 topological qubits on a chip designed, Microsoft says, to scale to 1 million qubits.
  • Quantum processors are kept within a few hundredths of a degree of absolute zero — colder than deep space — to protect their fragile states.

Every computer you have ever used thinks in bits: tiny switches that are either 0 or 1. A quantum computer thinks in qubits — units that can be 0, 1, or a blend of both at once. That single difference unlocks a completely different kind of computing, one that scientists have chased for 40 years. In the space of a few months in 2024 and 2025, two very different machines suggested the chase is finally getting somewhere.

Bits versus qubits

A qubit exploits two quantum effects. Superposition lets it hold a combination of 0 and 1 rather than one or the other. Entanglement links qubits so that measuring one instantly tells you something about its partners, however far apart they are. Carefully choreographed, these effects let a quantum computer explore certain problems — simulating molecules, optimizing logistics, factoring large numbers — along paths no ordinary computer can follow. The catch is fragility: heat, vibration or a stray electromagnetic nudge destroys the quantum state, a failure called decoherence. That is why quantum processors run inside elaborate refrigerators cooled to within a few hundredths of a degree of absolute zero — colder than deep space.

The real enemy: errors

Because qubits are so fragile, they make mistakes constantly. The field's central challenge is quantum error correction: spreading one reliable "logical" qubit across many physical qubits, so errors can be spotted and fixed mid-calculation. For three decades, theory said this only works if physical error rates fall below a threshold — and that adding more qubits should then reduce errors, not multiply them. Proving it in practice was the milestone everyone was waiting for.

Willow: errors that shrink as you scale

Google delivered that proof on December 9, 2024. Its Willow chip, carrying 105 superconducting qubits, ran error-correction experiments in which larger qubit grids performed better than smaller ones — the first clear demonstration of "below threshold" operation, published in Nature. Willow also ran a benchmark calculation in under five minutes that Google estimates would take the fastest classical supercomputer 10 septillion years. That benchmark is a contrived test, not a useful task, and Google says so itself. The error-correction result is the one physicists celebrated: it showed the main obstacle to large quantum machines can, in principle, be engineered away.

Majorana 1: protection built into the hardware

Microsoft is betting on fixing errors at the source. On February 19, 2025, it unveiled Majorana 1, a chip built around a new material it calls a topoconductor — layers of indium arsenide and aluminum engineered to host exotic quasiparticles called Majorana zero modes. Information stored in these "topological" qubits is spread out in a way that should make it naturally resistant to local noise — error protection built into the physics rather than patched on with software. The first chip holds eight topological qubits; Microsoft says the architecture is designed to scale to one million qubits on a single chip, and it is building a fault-tolerant prototype under a DARPA program. Independent experts urge healthy caution — a fully demonstrated topological qubit remains contested ground — but the approach is now a serious contender.

What quantum computers will — and won't — do

Quantum computers will not replace your phone or laptop; for everyday tasks, classical machines win easily. Their promise is narrower and deeper: modeling molecules for new medicines and fertilizers, designing better batteries and catalysts, and tackling optimization problems that choke today's supercomputers. After Willow and Majorana 1, the honest summary is this — the physics works, the engineering is brutal, and the race to the first truly useful quantum machine is now a contest of scale.
