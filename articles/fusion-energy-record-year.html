Fusion Energy's Record Run: Power Like the Sun

By Global Social Hub · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
Fusion powers the Sun by squeezing light atoms together. Reproducing that process on Earth is one of the great engineering challenges of the century. (Image: Unsplash)

Key Facts

  • On April 7, 2025, the U.S. National Ignition Facility produced a record 8.6 megajoules of fusion energy from 2.08 megajoules of laser energy — a target gain of 4.13 (LLNL).
  • The first-ever fusion ignition, in December 2022, produced 3.15 megajoules from 2.05 megajoules of laser input.
  • ITER, the international reactor under construction in France, is designed to produce 500 megawatts of fusion power from 50 megawatts of heating — a gain of 10.
  • Under ITER's current schedule, research operations begin in 2034 and full deuterium-tritium operation in 2039.
  • Private fusion companies have attracted about $9.8 billion in total funding, according to the Fusion Industry Association (2025).

Every second, the Sun converts 600 million tons of hydrogen into light and heat by crushing atoms together until they fuse. For seventy years, scientists have chased the same trick on Earth, lured by a fuel supply — seawater and lithium — that could power civilization for millions of years. The chase has a reputation for always being thirty years away. But the last few years have delivered something new: records, real ones, arriving one after another.

The laser that beat the Sun's trick

The headline results come from the National Ignition Facility (NIF) in California, where 192 laser beams converge on a fuel pellet smaller than a peppercorn. In December 2022, NIF achieved ignition for the first time in history: 2.05 megajoules of laser energy produced 3.15 megajoules of fusion energy — more out than in, at the target. The team has repeated ignition many times since, steadily raising the yield. On April 7, 2025, according to Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, a shot produced a record 8.6 megajoules — over four times the laser energy delivered, a target gain of 4.13.

Honesty requires a caveat, and fusion scientists are the first to state it: NIF's lasers draw far more electricity from the wall than the shot returns, and NIF is a research facility, not a power plant prototype. Ignition proved the physics works. Turning it into electricity is an engineering problem — a different, though now plausible, challenge.

The giant in France

The other road to fusion runs through Cadarache, in southern France, where 35 nations are jointly building ITER — the largest fusion experiment in history. ITER uses the tokamak design: powerful magnets hold a ring of hydrogen plasma heated to 270 million degrees Fahrenheit (150 million degrees Celsius), hotter than the Sun's core. Its goal is unambiguous — 500 megawatts of fusion power from 50 megawatts of heating, a tenfold gain, sustained for minutes at a time.

ITER has also become a byword for delay. Under its revised baseline, adopted in 2024, research operations are now set to begin in 2034, with full deuterium-tritium fuel operation in 2039. Yet the machine itself is visibly taking shape: installation of the nine giant vacuum-vessel sector modules began in April 2025, and assembly has recently run ahead of the revised schedule. ITER will never sell a watt of electricity. Its job is to prove a burning plasma can be controlled at power-plant scale — the knowledge every future reactor will be built on.

The startups join the race

What has truly changed this decade is who else is playing. According to the Fusion Industry Association's 2025 report, 53 private fusion companies have now attracted about $9.8 billion in funding — $2.64 billion of it in a single year — betting that modern superconducting magnets, better computing and private-sector speed can beat the big public projects to a working reactor. Commonwealth Fusion Systems is assembling its SPARC reactor in Massachusetts, designed to demonstrate net fusion energy in a machine far smaller than ITER. Most companies surveyed say they expect to deliver power to the grid in the 2030s — a claim to treat as ambition rather than fact, but one now backed by serious hardware.

Why this decade is different

Fusion's eternal joke — always thirty years away — assumed progress would crawl. Instead, the field now has demonstrated ignition, a reactor-scale experiment under construction, and a private industry racing both. Obstacles remain formidable: materials that survive years of neutron bombardment, breeding the tritium fuel, and doing it all cheaply enough to compete. But for the first time, the question has shifted from whether fusion can work to who will make it work first — and that is what a record run looks like.
