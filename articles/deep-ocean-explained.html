The Deep Ocean: Earth's Last Great Frontier

By Global Social Hub · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
A catshark drifts beneath a jellyfish in the dark ocean depths, lit by a research vehicle. Below about 650 feet (200 m), sunlight gives out entirely. (Image: NOAA, via Wikimedia Commons)

Key Facts

  • The deep ocean starts about 650 feet (200 m) down, where sunlight fades — and it is the largest habitat on Earth by volume.
  • Challenger Deep, the deepest point, lies about 6.8 miles (10,935 m) down in the Mariana Trench, under pressure more than 1,000 times that at the surface.
  • Only about 27% of the seafloor had been mapped to modern standards by mid-2025 (Seabed 2030).
  • The deepest fish ever filmed was a snailfish at 27,349 feet (8,336 m) in the Izu-Ogasawara Trench near Japan.
  • In March 2025, a Schmidt Ocean Institute expedition filmed a living colossal squid in its natural habitat for the first time — 100 years after the species was identified.

There is a world on Earth where the sun never shines, temperatures hover just above freezing, and the pressure would crush a car — yet it is full of life. It begins a few hundred feet below the waves and extends down nearly 7 miles (11 km). The deep ocean is the largest habitat on the planet, and we have mapped it less precisely than the surface of the Moon.

A world in layers

Oceanographers divide the water column into zones. Below the sunlit surface layer, the twilight zone stretches from about 650 to 3,300 feet (200 to 1,000 m), where the last blue light fades. Then come the midnight zone, pitch black and near 39°F (4°C), the abyssal zone, and finally the hadal zone — named for Hades — inside deep trenches below 19,700 feet (6,000 m). The very bottom is Challenger Deep in the Pacific's Mariana Trench, about 6.8 miles (10,935 m) down. If Mount Everest were dropped into it, its summit would still be more than a mile underwater. Down there, the water pressure exceeds 1,000 times the pressure at the surface — comparable, as NOAA often puts it, to carrying the weight of about 50 jumbo jets.

Life that makes its own light

With no sunlight, there is no photosynthesis, so most deep life runs on leftovers: a slow rain of dead plankton and waste from above, called marine snow. Around hydrothermal vents, though, entire ecosystems run on chemistry instead — microbes harvest energy from minerals in scalding vent water, and crabs, worms and shrimp graze on them, a food web with no connection to the sun. Most deep animals make their own light: bioluminescence is used to hunt, hide and communicate in the dark. Life also adapts to pressure in stranger ways — the deepest fish ever filmed, a snailfish at 27,349 feet (8,336 m) in the Izu-Ogasawara Trench near Japan, survives there with a soft, scaleless body and a flexible skull that lets the pressure pass through it.

March 2025: the colossal squid, at last

Some deep residents are legendary. The colossal squid — among the largest invertebrates on Earth, with eyes the size of dinner plates — was identified in 1925 from remains found in a sperm whale's stomach, yet no one had ever filmed one alive in its own world. That changed on March 9, 2025. During an Ocean Census expedition near the remote South Sandwich Islands in the South Atlantic, the Schmidt Ocean Institute's remotely operated vehicle SuBastian, working from the research vessel Falkor (too), captured a juvenile colossal squid — about 1 foot (30 cm) long and nearly transparent — drifting 1,968 feet (600 m) down. Experts later confirmed the footage: the first confirmed look at this animal alive at home, exactly 100 years after its discovery.

The map is mostly blank

For all our technology, the deep remains astonishingly unknown. The international Seabed 2030 project reports that only about 27% of the seafloor had been mapped to modern standards by mid-2025 — the rest is known only from rough satellite estimates. Seeing is rarer still: a 2025 analysis of six decades of deep dives concluded that humans have visually observed less than 0.001% of the deep seafloor. Every serious expedition finds species new to science. That is why oceanographers call the deep ocean Earth's last great frontier: it regulates our climate, feeds billions of people and hides medicines and minerals we have barely begun to catalog — and most of it has never been seen by human eyes.
