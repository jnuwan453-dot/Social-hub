How Starlink Works: Internet From Orbit

By The Civic Brief · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
A SpaceX Falcon rocket on its way up. Starlink satellites ride to orbit in batches, then spread out into the constellation. (Image: Unsplash)

Key Facts

  • Starlink satellites orbit about 340 miles (550 km) above Earth — roughly 65 times closer than traditional geostationary internet satellites.
  • By the end of 2025, about 9,400 Starlink satellites were in orbit — more than half of all satellites then circling Earth (catalog data compiled by Jonathan McDowell).
  • SpaceX reported more than 8 million Starlink customers worldwide by late 2025.
  • About 650 Starlink satellites carry direct-to-cell equipment; T-Mobile's T-Satellite service, using them, launched commercially in the U.S. on July 23, 2025, starting with messaging and adding limited app data in October 2025.
  • Starlink satellites are designed to burn up completely in the atmosphere at the end of their roughly five-year service lives.

Satellite internet used to mean a dish pointed at a satellite parked 22,236 miles (35,786 km) above the equator — and a half-second lag on every click. Starlink, SpaceX's broadband network, took the opposite approach: put the satellites absurdly close, and build thousands of them. The result is the largest machine ever flown in orbit, and an internet connection that works in farmhouses, fishing boats and war zones alike.

Why low orbit changes everything

Starlink satellites fly in low Earth orbit, about 340 miles (550 km) up. Radio signals make the round trip to that altitude in a few thousandths of a second, so Starlink connections typically respond in about 20 to 60 milliseconds — comparable to wired broadband, and fast enough for video calls and online games. Traditional geostationary satellites, 65 times farther away, cannot beat roughly 600 milliseconds. The trade-off is coverage: a satellite at 340 miles sees only a small patch of Earth and races across the sky in minutes, so continuous service demands a vast relay team — a constellation.

How your request travels

At home, a flat Starlink dish — nicknamed "Dishy" — uses a phased-array antenna to electronically steer its beam and lock onto satellites overhead, no motors or manual aiming required. The satellite receives your request and routes it onward. Newer satellites carry laser links that pass data between spacecraft in orbit at light speed, then down to a ground station connected to the internet — or, near the poles and oceans, entirely through the laser mesh. Satellites hand your connection from one to the next as they pass, invisibly, every few minutes.

Building the fleet is an industrial operation in itself. Falcon 9 rockets carry Starlink satellites up in batches — around 20 or more of the current V2 Mini design per flight — and SpaceX launched more than 3,200 Starlink satellites in 2025 alone. By the end of that year, roughly 9,400 were in orbit, more than half of all satellites around Earth, serving over 8 million customers.

2025: the year Starlink reached ordinary phones

The network's newest trick needs no dish at all. Around 650 Starlink satellites carry direct-to-cell equipment — effectively cell towers in space that speak the same radio language as standard smartphones, using spectrum leased from partner carriers. In the United States, T-Mobile's T-Satellite service launched commercially on July 23, 2025, first offering text and picture messaging in areas with no ground coverage; limited data for selected apps followed on October 1, 2025. Voice calls are planned but were not yet available. For hikers, sailors and anyone caught in a disaster zone, the dead zone is beginning to shrink.

The costs of a crowded sky

Starlink's scale brings real concerns. Astronomers worry about satellite trails crossing telescope images and the growing brightness of the night sky; SpaceX has responded with darker coatings and sunshades, with mixed results. Orbit safety matters too — with tens of thousands of satellites planned across several companies' constellations, collision avoidance is a daily operational task, and each Starlink satellite is designed to deorbit and burn up completely within about five years of launch. Whether the benefits of global connectivity outweigh the costs is a live public debate — but the technology itself is no longer experimental. Internet from orbit is simply here.
