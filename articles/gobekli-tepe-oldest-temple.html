Göbekli Tepe: The Temple Older Than the Pyramids

By The Civic Brief · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
The main excavation area at Göbekli Tepe, near Şanlıurfa in southeastern Türkiye. (Image: Wikimedia Commons)

Key Facts

  • Göbekli Tepe stands about 10 miles (15 km) from Şanlıurfa in southeastern Türkiye; its oldest enclosures date to around 9600 BCE — roughly 11,500 years ago.
  • Its great T-shaped limestone pillars stand up to 18 feet (5.5 m) tall and weigh several tons each, carved with foxes, boars, cranes and other animals.
  • It predates Stonehenge by about 6,000 years and Egypt's Great Pyramid by about 7,000 years — and was built by hunter-gatherers before farming existed.
  • German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt recognized the site's importance in 1994; excavations began in 1995, and UNESCO made it a World Heritage Site in 2018.
  • In September 2025, Türkiye's Ministry of Culture and Tourism announced a life-size human statue found embedded in a wall between Structures B and D — interpreted as a votive offering.

On a limestone hill in southeastern Türkiye, archaeologists are digging up a problem for the history books. The stone circles of Göbekli Tepe were raised around 9600 BCE — roughly 11,500 years ago. That is thousands of years before the Egyptian pyramids, before Stonehenge, before writing, pottery or the wheel. Most startling of all, the builders appear to have been hunter-gatherers who had not yet learned to farm.

Stones that should not exist yet

The site consists of great circular and oval enclosures ringed by T-shaped limestone pillars, the largest standing about 18 feet (5.5 m) tall and weighing several tons. Many are carved in relief with wild animals — foxes, boars, snakes, cranes, scorpions — and some bear stylized arms and hands, suggesting the pillars themselves represent human figures. The stone was quarried from the plateau nearby, shaped with stone tools, and hauled into place by a society with no metal, no draft animals and no wheels.

Organizing that labor would have required feeding and coordinating hundreds of people. The German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt, who recognized the hill's significance in 1994 after earlier surveyors had dismissed it, argued that the effort of building Göbekli Tepe may itself have pushed these communities toward settled life — that the temple came first, and farming followed to support it. That idea remains debated, but the old textbook order — first agriculture, then villages, then monuments — no longer looks secure.

A site that was buried on purpose

Stranger still, the enclosures were eventually filled in and buried deliberately, which is why they survived so well. Only a fraction of the hill has been excavated since digging began in 1995; geophysical surveys show more enclosures still sleeping underground. Göbekli Tepe is also no longer seen as a lone marvel. It is the flagship of the Taş Tepeler — the "Stone Hills" — a cluster of related Neolithic sites in the region, including Karahan Tepe, where similar pillars and carvings have come to light in recent years.

The 2025 discovery: a figure hidden in a wall

Excavation today is led by Professor Necmi Karul of Istanbul University, and the finds keep coming. In September 2025, Türkiye's Ministry of Culture and Tourism announced that a life-size human statue had been uncovered lying horizontally inside a wall between two of the great enclosures, Structures B and D. The figure's head and torso are clearly formed, though its feet are missing. Researchers interpret the statue as a votive offering — an object placed inside the wall deliberately, perhaps to consecrate or protect the building. Similar statues are known from Karahan Tepe, but it is the first of its kind found at Göbekli Tepe itself.

Why it matters

Göbekli Tepe forces a humbling thought: complex art, architecture and organized ritual did not wait for cities or kings. The people who carved these pillars lived by hunting and gathering, yet they cooperated on a scale once thought impossible for their era. Recent research also complicates the picture of a pure ceremonial center, with growing evidence of dwellings and everyday life on the hill. Eleven and a half millennia later, the Stone Hills still have most of their secrets underground — and every season, the trowels keep rewriting the first chapter of civilization.
