CRISPR Explained: Editing the Code of Life

By The Civic Brief · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
Cells under a fluorescence microscope. Gene editing lets scientists rewrite the DNA inside cells like these — and in 2025 it was used to treat a single baby. (Image: Unsplash)

Key Facts

  • Your genome is a code of about 3 billion DNA letters — and a single wrong letter can cause a serious disease.
  • CRISPR was adapted from a bacterial immune system; the landmark Doudna and Charpentier paper appeared in 2012, and the pair won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2020.
  • The first CRISPR medicine, Casgevy, was approved by the FDA in December 2023, for sickle cell disease.
  • In 2025, baby KJ Muldoon became the first person treated with a personalized CRISPR therapy, designed for his exact mutation in about six months (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia / Penn Medicine).
  • KJ's disorder, CPS1 deficiency, affects roughly 1 in 1.3 million babies and is often fatal in infancy.

Every cell in your body carries the same instruction manual: a molecule of DNA written in about 3 billion chemical letters. When even one letter is wrong, the result can be a devastating disease. For most of history, doctors could only treat the symptoms of such genetic errors. CRISPR changed that — it gave scientists a way to find a single wrong letter in the manual and fix it.

Scissors borrowed from bacteria

CRISPR was not invented in a laboratory. It was discovered in bacteria, which use it as an immune system: when a virus attacks, the bacterium stores a snippet of the invader's DNA and later uses it to recognize and cut that DNA if the virus returns. In 2012, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier showed this system could be reprogrammed to cut any DNA sequence scientists chose — work that won them the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2020. The tool has two parts: a guide RNA that acts like a GPS, leading the machinery to one exact address in the genome, and a protein — usually Cas9 — that acts as scissors. A newer version, called base editing, works less like scissors and more like a pencil: instead of cutting DNA, it chemically rewrites a single letter, which is gentler and more precise.

From laboratory tool to approved medicine

The first CRISPR medicine reached patients in December 2023, when the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved Casgevy for sickle cell disease. Doctors remove a patient's own blood stem cells, edit them in the laboratory so they produce healthy hemoglobin, and return them to the body. The approach works, but it is slow, demanding and expensive — and it can only fix cells that can be taken out of the body. The bigger prize is editing DNA inside the body itself. That is exactly what happened in 2025.

A treatment built for one baby

KJ Muldoon was born in August 2024 with severe CPS1 deficiency, a genetic disorder so rare it affects roughly 1 in 1.3 million babies. His body could not clear ammonia, a toxic waste product of protein, and the buildup threatened his brain. Half of babies with the condition die in infancy. A team at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia and Penn Medicine, led by Rebecca Ahrens-Nicklas and Kiran Musunuru, proposed something never attempted: design a base-editing treatment for KJ's exact mutation — a drug for an audience of one. In about six months they designed, tested and manufactured it, delivering the editor to his liver inside tiny fat bubbles called lipid nanoparticles. KJ received three infusions between February and April 2025, starting at six months old. He tolerated them well, could eat more protein, and needed less medication. In June 2025, after more than 300 days in hospital, he went home. The case was published in The New England Journal of Medicine — the first documented personalized CRISPR treatment. His doctors stress it is too soon to call it a cure, but by early 2026 KJ was walking and talking.

What comes next

KJ's case is now reshaping regulation: in February 2026, the FDA joined his doctors in announcing a "plausible mechanism" framework intended to speed approval of one-off treatments for rare diseases, where large clinical trials are impossible. Challenges remain formidable — delivering editors to organs beyond the liver, keeping treatments affordable, and proving edits never land in the wrong place. But the direction is clear. Medicine spent a century treating diseases shared by millions. CRISPR has shown it can also treat a disease carried by exactly one child.
