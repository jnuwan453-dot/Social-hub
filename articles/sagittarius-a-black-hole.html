The Monster at the Center: Sagittarius A*

By Global Social Hub · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
Deep space. At the center of our own galaxy, a supermassive black hole bends light and spacetime around it. (Image: Unsplash)

Key Facts

  • Sagittarius A* is about 4 million times the mass of the Sun, packed into a region roughly 15 million miles (24 million km) across.
  • It sits about 27,000 light-years from Earth, at the exact center of the Milky Way.
  • The Event Horizon Telescope captured the first image of it on May 12, 2022.
  • A star called S2 orbits it once every 16 years, reaching about 17 million mph — nearly 3% of the speed of light.
  • The 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics honoured the scientists who proved the dark object is a supermassive black hole.

Every star in the night sky, including our Sun, is slowly orbiting something. At the center of the Milky Way galaxy — about 27,000 light-years away, hidden behind thick clouds of dust — sits Sagittarius A* (pronounced "A-star"), a supermassive black hole containing the mass of roughly 4 million suns. Nothing that gets too close ever comes back. And yet, in 2022, humanity took its photograph.

Weighing the invisible

Long before the image, astronomers knew something enormous was there. Teams led by Reinhard Genzel in Germany and Andrea Ghez in the United States spent decades tracking individual stars near the galactic center using infrared telescopes that can peer through the dust. One star, S2, completes a full orbit in just 16 years, swinging past the central object at up to 17 million mph — nearly 3% of the speed of light.

Only one explanation fits: those stars are orbiting an object millions of times heavier than the Sun, squeezed into a volume smaller than our solar system, that emits almost no light of its own. A black hole. The work earned Genzel and Ghez a share of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Photographing a shadow

A black hole itself emits no light, but gas spiralling into it heats to billions of degrees and glows. The Event Horizon Telescope (EHT) — a worldwide network of radio observatories working together as one Earth-sized telescope — set out to image that glowing ring and the dark shadow at its heart.

On May 12, 2022, the EHT released the result: a blurry orange ring of superheated gas surrounding a dark center — the shadow of Sagittarius A*. It was the second black hole ever imaged, after M87* in 2019, and the first look at our own galaxy's central monster. The two images look strikingly similar, exactly as Einstein's general theory of relativity predicts, even though Sagittarius A* is a thousand times smaller.

A surprisingly gentle giant

For all its mass, Sagittarius A* is currently quiet — astronomers call it a "starving" black hole, sipping gas rather than feasting. If it were actively devouring large amounts of material, the galactic center would blaze like a quasar. Evidence suggests it has woken up before: giant bubbles of gamma-ray light above and below the Milky Way, discovered by the Fermi telescope, may be the scars of an outburst a few million years ago.

That quietness is good news. At 27,000 light-years away, Sagittarius A* poses no threat to Earth — its gravity rules the galactic center, but our solar system orbits it in perfect safety, one revolution every 230 million years. The monster at the center is less a menace than an anchor: the gravitational heart around which our entire galaxy, and everything in it, gracefully turns.
