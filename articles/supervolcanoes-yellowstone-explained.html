Supervolcanoes Explained: The Giant Beneath Yellowstone

By Global Social Hub · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
The Grand Prismatic Spring in Yellowstone National Park — the colorful surface of one of the largest volcanic systems on Earth. (Image: Wikimedia Commons)

Key Facts

  • Yellowstone's caldera measures about 45 miles by 30 miles (72 by 48 km) — the scar left by its last super-eruption, 640,000 years ago.
  • A super-eruption (VEI-8) ejects at least 240 cubic miles (1,000 km³) of material; Yellowstone has produced three in the past 2.1 million years.
  • Yellowstone typically records 1,500–2,500 small earthquakes a year (USGS).
  • The magma beneath Yellowstone is mostly solid — only about 5 to 15% molten, far below the roughly 50% scientists say an eruption would require (USGS).
  • Latest USGS/YVO status (November 2, 2026 update): alert level NORMAL, aviation code GREEN — activity at background levels.

Beneath the geysers, hot springs and bison of Yellowstone National Park lies one of the largest volcanoes on Earth — so large that its crater is not a mountain at all, but a depression about 45 miles long and 30 miles wide (72 by 48 km). Yellowstone is a supervolcano, and its past eruptions rank among the most powerful events in the planet's recent history. It is also one of the most closely watched volcanoes on Earth, and the scientists watching it are calm. Here is why.

What makes a volcano "super"

Geologists reserve the term supervolcano for eruptions rated 8 on the Volcanic Explosivity Index — the top of the scale. A VEI-8 eruption ejects at least 240 cubic miles (1,000 km³) of rock and ash, enough to bury a large city under hundreds of feet of debris. Such eruptions are so violent that the ground collapses into the emptied magma chamber, leaving a vast crater called a caldera instead of a cone. Yellowstone's caldera was blasted out 640,000 years ago. The engine beneath it is a hotspot: a plume of hot rock rising from deep inside Earth that has fed eruptions for millions of years.

Three eruptions that rewrote a continent

Yellowstone has produced three super-eruptions: 2.1 million years ago, 1.3 million years ago, and 640,000 years ago. The most recent, the Lava Creek eruption, expelled roughly 240 cubic miles (1,000 km³) of material — about 1,000 times the volume of Mount St. Helens' famous 1980 eruption — and spread a layer of ash across much of North America. An eruption on that scale today would devastate the region and cool the global climate for years. It is a sobering thought — and a misleading one, if it leads you to expect a repeat any time soon.

"Overdue" is a myth

Because the three eruptions were spaced roughly 600,000 to 800,000 years apart, headlines sometimes declare Yellowstone "overdue." Volcanoes do not keep schedules, and the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS) is blunt: there is no sign an eruption is coming. For a super-eruption, a magma chamber must be at least about 50% molten; seismic imaging shows Yellowstone's is mostly solid crystal, only about 5 to 15% melt, stored in separate pockets. In 2025, researchers publishing in Nature imaged a volatile-rich cap of magma about 2.4 miles (3.9 km) down that steadily leaks gas upward through the park's hot springs — in effect, a lid that breathes, releasing pressure rather than storing it. Scientists also expect years of unmistakable warning — intense earthquake swarms and rapid ground uplift — before any large eruption, none of which is happening.

Watched around the clock

The Yellowstone Volcano Observatory (YVO), run by the USGS with the University of Utah and the park, tracks the volcano with seismometers, GPS stations and gas sensors, and publishes a public update every month. The picture they paint is of a restless but normal system: 1,500 to 2,500 small earthquakes a year is typical, and the caldera floor has been slowly sinking since about 2015, with seasonal pauses. In its November 2, 2026 update, YVO kept Yellowstone at alert level NORMAL and aviation color code GREEN, reporting 66 earthquakes in October 2026 — the largest just magnitude 2.0 — and no significant ground movement. The realistic hazards, scientists stress, are local ones: strong earthquakes and sudden steam-driven hydrothermal explosions, like the one that startled visitors at Biscuit Basin in July 2024.

Yellowstone deserves its fame: a landscape of more than 10,000 hydrothermal features and about 500 geysers — over half of all the geysers on Earth — floating on a giant of a volcano. But fame invites fear, and the instruments tell a quieter story: a system being watched hour by hour, behaving exactly as it has for decades.
