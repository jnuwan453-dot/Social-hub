K2-18b: A Distant World and the Search for Life

By Global Social Hub · November 26, 2026 · 4 min read
Deep space. K2-18b lies 124 light-years away — far too distant to photograph directly, so astronomers read its atmosphere in starlight. (Image: Unsplash)

Key Facts

  • K2-18b orbits a red dwarf star about 124 light-years away in the constellation Leo, inside the star's habitable zone.
  • It is a sub-Neptune: about 2.6 times Earth's radius and 8.6 times its mass, with no true equivalent in our solar system.
  • In 2023, the James Webb Space Telescope detected methane and carbon dioxide in its atmosphere.
  • In April 2025, a University of Cambridge team reported possible dimethyl sulfide (DMS) and/or dimethyl disulfide (DMDS) at about 3-sigma significance — debated and unconfirmed, and below the 5-sigma standard for a discovery.
  • Independent reanalyses of the same data, including a 2025 study led by Renyu Hu, found no DMS signal and describe a water-rich interior instead.

Of the thousands of planets discovered beyond our solar system, few have generated as much argument as K2-18b. It is a world with no counterpart at home: bigger than Earth, smaller than Neptune, orbiting a cool red dwarf star 124 light-years away in the constellation Leo. It also sits in its star's habitable zone, where temperatures could allow liquid water to exist. In 2025, it became the center of the most contested claim in the search for life — a claim that remains, for now, unproven.

Reading an atmosphere from 124 light-years away

Nobody has ever seen K2-18b directly. Discovered by NASA's Kepler space telescope in 2015, it reveals itself when it crosses in front of its star, blocking a fraction of the starlight. A sliver of that light filters through the planet's atmosphere, and molecules in the air leave fingerprints — faint dips at specific wavelengths. The James Webb Space Telescope is sensitive enough to read those fingerprints, a technique called transmission spectroscopy.

In 2023, a team led by Nikku Madhusudhan at the University of Cambridge reported that Webb had detected methane and carbon dioxide in K2-18b's atmosphere, with little ammonia. That combination, they argued, fits a "Hycean" world — their term for a planet covered by a global ocean beneath a hydrogen-rich atmosphere. The same data held a weaker hint of something far more provocative.

The 2025 claim: a whiff of a biological gas

The molecule was dimethyl sulfide, or DMS. On Earth, DMS is produced by living organisms — mainly marine phytoplankton — and has no known significant non-biological source on our planet. In April 2025, Madhusudhan's team published new Webb observations made with a different instrument, MIRI, reporting evidence for DMS and/or a related molecule, dimethyl disulfide (DMDS), which the data could not tell apart. The University of Cambridge described the results as the strongest hints yet of possible biological activity beyond the solar system.

The team itself was careful: the signal stood at roughly 3-sigma significance. In plain terms, there is about a 0.3 percent chance that noise alone would produce a signal that strong — suggestive, but well short of the 5-sigma threshold scientists require to declare a discovery. Madhusudhan publicly urged skepticism about his own result and said more Webb observations would be needed to confirm it.

The pushback

Other astronomers were not persuaded. Several independent groups reanalyzed the same Webb data and found that the apparent DMS signal weakened or disappeared depending on how the data were processed and which molecules the models were allowed to include. Critics also noted that DMS has been detected in places with no life at all, including a comet and interstellar space, so even a real detection would not automatically mean biology.

Then came new data. A 2025 study led by Renyu Hu combined additional Webb transit observations with earlier data and found robust methane and carbon dioxide — but no evidence of DMS or DMDS. Their conclusion: K2-18b has a water-rich interior, though whether that water forms a surface ocean or sits beneath a thick atmosphere is still unresolved.

What would settle it

The honest verdict is that K2-18b is a genuinely water-rich, temperate world — and that no one has yet demonstrated life there. Confirming or ruling out DMS will take many more hours of Webb observing time, better laboratory measurements of how these molecules absorb light, and eventually a new generation of telescopes designed to study Earth-like planets directly. The episode is not a failure of science; it is science working as intended — an extraordinary claim, tested in the open, by rivals with the same data.
